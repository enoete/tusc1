Define — What does mandatory mean, and why does it matter?
Define what you're arguing. Mandatory means required — not optional, not an extra. Every student, in every school, gets art and music as part of their education. Explain why that matters before you prove it.
The simple definition
"When I say mandatory, I mean required. Not a reward for good grades. Not something only certain schools offer. I mean that every child — no matter which school they attend, no matter their background — has the right to learn through art and music."
Why it works: clear, inclusive, and sets up the fairness argument.
The "whole child" definition
"Education is not just about what you know. It is about who you become. Art and music teach children to think creatively, to feel deeply, to communicate powerfully. Without them, we are only educating half a person."
Why it works: reframes the whole debate — not just about subjects, but about what kind of people we want to produce.
Evidence — Build your case
Use three or four strong points. Each one should answer the question: "Why should art and music be mandatory?" Use your own experience as a musician, facts, and real-world examples.
Evidence 1 — Brain development
"Research shows that students who study music and art perform better academically — in reading, in math, in problem-solving. Learning to read music develops the same part of the brain as learning to read words. Art teaches children to look carefully, to think critically, and to solve problems in ways no textbook can."
Why it works: directly answers the objection that art takes time away from "real" subjects.
Evidence 2 — Mental health and expression
"Many children struggle to express how they feel in words. Art and music give them another language — one that doesn't require a pen and paper. Studies have found that creative activities reduce stress, anxiety, and depression in young people. In a world where mental health is a growing crisis, we cannot afford to take creativity out of schools."
Why it works: connects to a topic the audience and judges care about — children's wellbeing.

Why it works: your story is your strongest evidence. No one can argue with what you've lived.

Fixing Last Time's Mistakes

Standing still

When you practise at home, put a piece of tape on the floor. Stand on it. Every time you feel the urge to move, plant your feet back on the tape. Your hands can move. Your eyes can move. Your feet stay still.

Eye contact

When you practise, put three objects in the room at different positions — left, centre, right. A stuffed animal, a cup, a book. Practise moving your eyes between them as you speak. On presentation day, replace those objects with real faces.

Your introduction

Say your name, your title, and your argument before anything else. Write these three sentences. Say them 20 times until you cannot forget them. This is the one thing you cannot wing.

Your closing

Write your last three sentences. Memorise them exactly. When you reach them on the day, slow down even more. Pause after your final sentence. Then say "Thank you" clearly, and stop. Do not add anything after "Thank you." That is your full stop.
